## The problem: zero onboarding strategy, and two weeks to create one

This is a classic case of what we call “an ambiguous problem”. Right after joining the Growth Design Team at MasterClass, I was asked to develop a design strategy, plan, or concept for the mobile app onboarding.

The mobile engineering lead told me that “design was a blocker”, but the reality was that the team was stretched thin working on many growth initiatives, and no one had defined an experiments roadmap for the app onboarding. When engineers neared the end of an experiment, someone would jump in with a one-off idea to try next. My task was to create a longer-term, cohesive vision.
And I had two weeks to do that.


## The analysis: if users know only your brand, and not your product, they won’t know what they should be paying for

MasterClass invested heavily in top-of-funnel marketing, but overexposing users to instructor-focused commercials had created bias. Visitors landed on MasterClass with a mental model that clashed with the product’s reality. Potential members were drawn to the glossy visuals, the instructors’ popularity and artistry, and the premium look and feel. The message was entirely aspirational, overlooking the product’s features. The users’ decision to pay was fully anchored to the instructors’ appeal.

I launched an unmoderated test to observe users going from installing the app to a free guest exploration, which was the experience at the time and included no onboarding. This quick research showed that free exploration didn’t explain what membership paid for beyond access to video content. The interface copy often assumed familiarity with the platform; for example, it referred to “Sessions”, the newly launched hands-on format, without explaining it. The only video content prospective users could play were Trailers, which felt anticlimactic and repeated the commercials they had already watched. Meanwhile, valuable features that could influence purchase decisions, such as Audio Only Mode, were completely hidden behind the paywall. As a result, many users concluded that MasterClass “didn’t fit their lifestyle” as a learning tool or wasn’t worth the money for just that one instructor’s class they were curious about.

The goal for my onboarding strategy was to change prospect users’ mental model by the end of the experience. To convert them into members, they needed to understand that MasterClass was more than just “celebrities”; it was a place with tools and structure for learning.


## The design process: setting goals, understanding requirements

Before getting to the board, I needed to think strategically and establish the goals.

Firstly, I needed to understand users’ goals when onboarding to MasterClass. Nearly all prospective users who installed the app had watched a MasterClass commercial. They were motivated by curiosity and ambition, and were lifelong learners eager to nurture their own growth. But what would they think was worth their money? Previous research showed that to commit, they wanted to know how large the catalog was (and whether it could last through the yearly subscription) and how MasterClass could fit their busy schedules.

After that, I asked what MasterClass’s business goal for the first-day experience was. It was obvious but vague: convert as many users as possible by the end of the first session. The yearly membership meant the retention cycle was long, and we couldn’t rely on the re-subscription rate. At the same time, Product hadn’t set a target; they just wanted a generic improvement. I could work with the idea of defining the baseline with the first experiment launch and building better hypotheses later.

Lastly, I needed to choose the story I wanted to tell with my onboarding design. Onboarding needs a narrative, an arc building a compelling promise, and ends in a call-to-action to convert. If the arguments are convincing, the chance of a conversion lift increases. My idea was to educate users about what made MasterClass worth subscribing to while giving them a taste of how learning happened on the platform. I chose short video to connect to both MasterClass’s main format and the familiar social media pattern.

There were just a few requirements to consider:

- Users had to go through the Plans screen right after the launcher. This was common since some users convert right away, and we don’t want to slow them down with unnecessary onboarding.
- Personalization tested well on the web, where users picked their interests and saw content relevant to their goals. I decided to expand this step by asking about their learning style and which devices they planned to use.


## The solution: a three-part onboarding with a modular twist

I split the onboarding experience into 3 parts:

1. The first part was the personalization. I had extensive experience in designing personalized mobile onboarding, and I knew users like to answer questions about their preferences.
2. The second part was a series of clips from classes, from the categories users chose during the personalization step. That would give them a taste of a real class, not a trailer, on topics they cared about.
3. The last part would still be made of clips, but educating users about MasterClass features. I picked the Audio Only Mode, the Sessions hands-on format, and the coursebook guides.

At the end, users would see the Plans and pricing screen again before moving on to their Home. Because I knew the current Home was letting down users who needed to explore for longer, I had ideas for reusing some of these concepts and modules to create a better post-onboarding experience.

## The final experience


### Before onboarding

#### Primary and secondary actions on the launcher screen
Redesigning the launcher wasn’t part of this project, yet the team kept changing ideas on gating the app and asking users to sign up or letting them explore without an account. I proposed keeping both options on the screen and running an A/B test by swapping the primary and secondary order to understand how order and visual treatment affected users’ choices. More important data would connect this initial choice to conversion.

#### An alternative layout for Plans
This was another sticky growth touchpoint. The last version was a long, vertically scrolling screen that wasn’t satisfying and hadn’t improved conversion much. We discussed other options, and I came up with a horizontal-scrolling carousel with small cards highlighting each plan’s main characteristic. After the launcher, the Plans screen slides up like a full-screen overlay; no plan is selected. Users who wanted to move to pricing had to select a plan and then continue (2 taps), while closing required one tap on the “Close” icon.

To connect these steps to the real onboarding, I maintained the original quote + spinner screen.


### Onboarding Part I: personalization

Based on my experience designing onboarding at Evernote, personalization is half about collecting information to tailor the experience to users’ preferences and half about creating a state of mind in which they feel comfortable and intrigued. People like to share about themselves, organize and decorate their personal space, even if it’s just through answering questions. Personalization creates the expectation of a more individualized experience, making the promise more compelling.

The logic for the first question was already built for the web client experiment, so engineering effort would be low. Because the categories chosen here would later be used to select class clips and organize content on the Home screen, users could not skip this question. Personalization required selecting at least 3 categories.

The second question didn’t change the rest of the onboarding. Getting that information could help us understand our audience later, but the real intention was to highlight that MasterClass had content for each learning style, showing the platform’s adaptability.

Similarly, the third question aimed to shape the user’s mental model rather than personalization. While working on Evernote's onboarding, I knew that asking about device use would also inform users about available apps and could boost multi-device adoption, which usually positively impacts activation and conversion.


### Onboarding Part II: clips from classes

The UI I designed blended familiar short-form video patterns from Instagram and TikTok with MasterClass content and branding. Users would instantly know how to move forward, backward, pause, and stop these reels. Clips already existed on MasterClass but were only available to paying subscribers. The good part was we had a library of clips, each connected to the standard Categories used for personalization. We could choose specific clips or Instructors for this experience or select them at random.

#### Secondary CTAs: bookmarking and exploring a Class’s lessons
I intended this concept and prototype to be as wide as possible, including many ideas we could later pull out and turn into individual experiments. I included examples for two secondary routes to engagement: bookmarking a clip and exploring the lessons breakdown for a Class. I knew building these alternative paths would increase scope, but the goal was not to show an MVP, but to provide food for thought to create a vision and roadmap.

In the prototype, tapping the bookmark icon triggers a sign-up screen, while following the “Explore this class” prompt opens a drawer with the lessons breakdown component we already have elsewhere on the platform. Spending more time consuming content, branching out, and exploring makes the onboarding more immersive and less risky than free, unstructured exploration.


### Onboarding Part III: shifting to features education

On the third Class clip, an overlay invites the user to interact with an audio-only toggle. Audio-only mode was a game-changer for retention: subscribers could keep a lesson playing when leaving the house or start a new one when going for a run. It made MasterClass a learning-on-the-go tool, which was exactly what they meant with “fitting their lifestyle”.

Combining the clip with feature education made for a smoother transition. But in the spirit of using video language throughout this onboarding, I created brief, simple animations for the rest of this section. This part was not personalized: no matter which clip was the 3rd and final, it would always be paired with the audio-only interactive toggle and followed by the Sessions presentation and the Guidebooks reel. This meant creating the audio-mode UI and only 2 animations, a task I was capable of carrying out myself.


### A modular design for progressive A/B testing

The prototype I shared visualized one possible version of the onboarding, the most expansive and complex. But my plan was to start small, and then work our way into a longer flow, using experimentation to find the right balance of the different modules and ideas.

Personalization questions, class clips, and feature reels were “knobs” for regulating the experience’s volume. We could expand or contract sections, leaning more towards personalization, or even breaking the division of type by mixing and randomizing class clips and feature reels. The goal was to find what led to more engagement and conversion, rather than replicating a specific version of these designs.

## Continuous onboarding through Home personalization

The current home experience was minimal to a fault: rows of carousels with Classes organized by overall popularity, and paywalls that blocked access to any content beyond trailers. Prospect members couldn’t save a class for later or set up their learning environment.
A more welcoming Home screen could lead to a longer first session and, with it, more chances of conversion. I could think of two easy ways to do this:

1. Creating a row of personalized Class clips and using it as the 1st top carousel on the Home.  
2. Personalize every carousel and section below by matching content to the chosen Categories.


## Conclusions

In the end, Design wasn’t a blocker, and the mobile engineering team couldn’t get to this vision right away. While it waited to pick up the first experiment on the list, MasterClass restructured the entire company and product team and moved away from prioritizing onboarding.

Reflecting on my work today, I know the full onboarding shown in my prototype could have worked or not, but the goal wasn’t really to achieve that exact combination of elements. Concepts such as using clips for feature education, or personalizing the onboarding content or the Home, had longer-term value and could have generated more and different solutions. I think the impact of my work showed up as trust that the Growth Design team and I could deliver bigger solutions by approaching design holistically.

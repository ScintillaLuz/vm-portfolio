## The problem: upgrading an old-school SEO strategy

Thoughtfully written, MasterClass Articles were brief pieces covering a wide range of topics, always matching a specific domain or category of a MasterClass class or instructor. The company’s initial SEO strategy focused on publishing as many “MasterClass Articles” as possible.
This SEO style generated little ROI. Acting as isolated entry points with little connection to one another or to the broader product, they drove conversions by volume rather than by quality. They all shared a single simplistic template filled with Instructor carousels, class video embeds, and subscription banners that read like ads and disrupted the narrative flow.

There was no reliable engagement tracking because the many buttons, links, and images on the page led to different pages, making it impossible to establish a baseline for metrics. The new SEO Product Manager inherited the Articles and a team of writers, and I was brought in as the SEO Growth Senior Designer. He restructured the team and set a new long-term goal: to elevate MasterClass’s SEO editorial content, turn Articles into a reliable acquisition channel, and integrate them into the courses’ supporting material.


## The project: launching a new SEO format

MasterClass instructors are well-known public figures, and Google searches for their names generate high daily traffic. The Product Manager wanted to capitalize on this potential and capture fans’ attention with a new article type: the Instructor Pages. The goal was to connect the Instructor Pages more closely with MasterClass’s offering and achieve a top-of-SERP ranking. My task was to design the new template from scratch and create a trackable funnel where every CTA was tested and weighted.


## The research: using co-creation for pattern evaluation

The Senior UX Researcher suggested conducting a co-creation exercise to understand how users reacted to different design patterns and what flow they expected.

To prepare for these moderated sessions, I defined the content outline for this article type: which sections would build the narrative about the Instructor, which accessory elements, such as quotes and aside boxes, could be included, and which MasterClass content and CTAs to use. The result was a Figma file containing a sequence of black-and-white design patterns, just above wireframe level, representing sections and modules of the hypothetical page.

We organized testing into two rounds. First, users evaluated which sections they wanted on the page and assessed pattern alternatives. For example, they chose whether to include images and, if so, they could pick either a single image or a gallery, as well as choosing which gallery type from the wireframe options in Figma. At the end, we showed users the full page with their chosen sections and patterns, and asked them to rearrange them into their ideal flow.

The main insights were:
• Users preferred a linear narrative, with sections introduced by headings
• Awards and achievements helped readers determine the instructor’s authority
• Biographical details were expected, but they only needed a few
• Curiosity was driven by a deep dive into the instructor’s craft and techniques
• On the opening hero, no one liked stiff headshots and preferred a shot of the instructor in action
• Users preferred to see just one video at a time; video carousels were too overwhelming
• They all appreciated getting detailed information about a class with its lessons’ breakdown
• Everyone wanted an exhaustive list of what the subscription price included


## My process: design principles and goals

The Product Manager wanted the pages to be so stunning and engaging that Instructors would want to share them with their fans. In our initial discussions, we agreed to create the kind of article that all teams would be proud to promote across the MasterClass site and apps, rather than hiding it in the “SEO tricks” space.

To succeed, this page needed to be a seamless, immersive reading experience where calls to action fit logically into the article’s narrative flow. The instructor’s story was structured as a funnel, building users’ curiosity until they reached the paywalled content and membership offer. The goal was to provide the information users sought first, then promote the instructor’s method and class, and finally tie everything back to MasterClass.

If the project proved effective, the Product Manager and I discussed the possibility of using the Instructor Pages’ template to revamp all the other MasterClass Articles.

## Visual style: finding a voice, defining a system

From a visual design standpoint, I defined my goals as balancing creativity and innovation while reducing production effort:

1. I aimed to define a unique style for the graphic and photographic elements that would make these pages recognizable while fitting the brand’s visual language and tone of voice.
2. I needed to avoid the classic MasterClass headshots, which users didn’t appreciate in articles, but still rely as much as possible on existing assets.
3. I wanted the merchandising process to be scalable so we could easily produce 50 or 100 pages without support from outside the SEO team.

Ultimately, we settled on a stylized look, a simple headline that didn’t require dynamic updates, a white background that contrasts with the platform pages’ black background, and a simple palette with 4 colors and geometric shapes rotating in the instructor list. I chose photographs from class shots, all material we had in abundance.


## Final layout: deep dive, section by section

For the design patterns, scalability was key. I aimed to design modules that could be modified, substituted, swapped, or expanded without disrupting the page’s balance. After launch, we could fine-tune details based on data. I already had wireframe patterns from the co-creation exercise, so I only needed to polish them to get the hi-fi version of the page. From top to bottom, here are some quick design considerations about the choices I’ve made.

### The landing experience: constructing an impactful first impression

The goal of the top of the page is to persuade users to keep scrolling. The hero photograph and the quote set the tone and visually elevate the page to a premium space, which matches MasterClass’s brand. The awards section confirms the instructor’s authority in their domain; the index serves as a scannable overview that helps readers assess content quality and jump to other sections. The video icons in the index indicate media content beyond words.

### The biography: the instructor’s personal story

Since we wanted to capture keyword searches for instructors’ names, biographies, and lives, this section was essential to reduce bounce. For this reason, we imagined its content to be unique, something that could compete with a Wikipedia page or the celeb’s personal website.

#### Design differences between desktop and mobile

On desktop, the index is a sidebar element that sticks to the top and follows the page's scroll. The CTA inside the sidebar is persistent. Because the desktop header has its own “View Plans” button, there’s some redundancy. However, the “Discover MasterClass” CTA in the sidebar links to a personalized pre-signup onboarding rather than the pricing page.

On mobile, this is the moment to nudge users to MasterClass quietly. I use motion to draw users’ attention to two CTAs: a basic bottom nav linking to Home, and a more indirect anchor link that scrolls to the class preview below the page.

### The instructor’s craft: decoding their method

This section carries the user one step further into the funnel. MasterClass’ content, for now, is woven into the fabric of the article by choosing one clip directly connected to the text. The journey goes from knowing more about the instructor to wanting to learn their craft. I’m using quotes to break the flow, and a single video section with a soft CTA.

### You can learn this method too: MasterClass’ promise

This is where we connect with the membership value proposition. The course video preview does the heavy lifting with the lessons breakdown. On desktop, I’m toggling the index to close and reveal a different side element with a MasterClass upsell. The animated change should catch the reader’s eye. On mobile, I make the floating bottom CTA disappear, since we’ve just reached the destination for that link.

Note how I offer a free download of the course Guidebook as bait in exchange for an email address. The Guidebooks were high-quality, instructive, and well-designed PDFs that members appreciated, but we were not yet using them to our advantage for acquisition.

### End of page experience

The modules at the end of the page are fairly standard for editorial design: one last upsell banner, links to other category-related Instructor Pages, and a list of MasterClass Articles in the same category. Few users are expected to arrive here, but there are SEO benefits to connecting Articles this way and providing a thoughtful way to navigate across Articles.

One note about desktop: the sticky sidebar stops scrolling before the sharing section, so I have plenty of space for a bolder banner that focuses more on the content, potentially featuring instructors in the same category as the featured instructor.


## Bonus: testing the template to retrofit older Articles

The legacy Articles lacked tracking, which was the main reason for suggesting a template change. If we couldn’t measure impact, it was better to restart and define a new baseline. We knew from qualitative testing that SEO Articles helped engage or re-engage users previously exposed to MasterClass promos, with some saying they discovered a class they didn’t know about and wanted to take through an Article. But because it was impossible to see the direct thread of conversion, changing the template was vital.

To demonstrate that it was economically viable to do the swap, I poured the Article’s content into the new layout, with the only change being a simpler hero section that uses the Article headline and subheading in place of the quote.

## An unexpected SEO insight from user research

I used unmoderated user testing to observe how users searched for celebrity biographies, which content made them bounce back to the SERP, and what was considered a good page. I gave users a list of MasterClass instructor names in a Google Slides doc and asked them to pick one they wanted to learn more about. The chosen name already had an old biographical MasterClass article online that could appear in the search results. Testers performed the search on their own, chose which links to click, and evaluated the pages based on how well they satisfied their need to know more about the person’s story.

Watching the recordings, I saw MasterClass links appear in their search results, sometimes in the second or third position. But all testers ignored these links and went straight to the celebrity’s official personal site, possibly biased by our request. They then usually backed out and clicked on a magazine interview article. They rated all these links inadequate. In the second part of the task, we gave them the old MasterClass article on their chosen celeb, and they found it well-written and informative. The content wasn’t the issue; the problem was that they didn’t see or consider the MasterClass link in their results.

I talked with my Product Manager because I suspected our link titles were the culprit. They were marketing-oriented, emphasizing MasterClass, the membership, and the value proposition rather than the article's topic. My Product Manager said he was puzzled by the “SEO mystery” around those old bio pages: they appeared frequently and ranked well, yet were rarely clicked. We now had our answer. Improving titles would become a priority for Instructor Pages.


## Conclusions

Soon after we presented this project, MasterClass restructured the entire Product org and dissolved the SEO team. The initiative was shelved before launch, and I was moved to the B2B Growth space. I can’t help but imagine how much easier it would be today to turn these ideas into a live template using modern AI tools. This would shift the discussion from “do we have the engineering resources to create a new template?” to “how fast can we see this live and start collecting data?”

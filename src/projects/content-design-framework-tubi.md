## The problem: asking new users to trust an algorithm that doesn’t know them

Everyone on the Product Team at Tubi recognized that our algorithm for creating content containers (the rows of movies and TV Series posters that make up most of a TV app’s interface) was working wonders for power users but fell short of being relevant to new users. Machine Learning knew Tubi’s catalog was a mix of niche and older titles and that new users had few parameters for proper personalization.

Users have been conditioned by social media to expect a highly personalized, polished experience they compare every new app to. The users we interviewed talked about “trusting the algorithm” on Netflix but were genuinely confused by the titles shown the first time they browsed Tubi. Our rows had broad titles like “Action”, “Horror”, and “Romance”. Except for the opening row, named “Recommended”, there was no way to know the rationale behind the selection. They asked, “Why would I get shown this?”

The Machine Learning team wanted to keep optimizing generic signals to improve first-visit engagement. But as a designer, I saw it differently: users know about algorithms but don’t see them as tech workers do. They assume intention when there isn’t any and don’t have the patience to wait for the next ML update to find relevant content. Some users we spoke to found it better to pick one piece of content they liked and use the related content row to navigate similar titles. This could pigeonhole them into one genre, making Tubi “the place where I go only when I want to see 90s nostalgia,” for example.

What if, in parallel, we used insights from our user research to change our approach to generating content containers?


## The research: watching behaviors and the audience’s tree of choice

In reality, picking up industry-wide signals to refine our containers would not have helped Machine Learning meet users where they were. Tubi containers were grouped into broad categories such as Comedy, Sci-fi, or Horror. The titles in these containers were chosen based on attributes that didn’t matter to new users, such as popularity on Tubi. Our catalog was skewed, with many niche, obscure, or older movies. Users interested in “Comedy” couldn’t understand why they saw Comedy titles they didn’t know or that didn’t seem to belong. They lacked context to understand our Machine Learning choices. With a TV app, we don’t question the algorithm’s inner workings: it either works or it doesn’t.

Tubi’s research on discovery habits challenged our bias that users looking for something new have no idea what they want to watch. While most users don’t have a specific title in mind and seek inspiration, they follow a decision-making process with distinct traits. Some of these we couldn’t leverage, such as current popularity or “hype” among coworkers, friends, and family. This is where Tubi differed from HBO Max or Netflix.

Not everything was beyond our reach. Other signals that lead to trust were ignored but could be designed for: mode, time, and vibe. These factors were part of the “audience’s tree of choice,” a sequence of questions users ask themselves after thinking, “I want to find something to watch.”

## The exercise: studying competitors, workshopping solutions

Inspired by my Netflix home carousel, I asked two colleagues to join a design exercise and collect examples from their Netflix carousel titles. We also gathered content carousel headings from non-streaming apps like Spotify and added new container ideas circulating within the company.
We spread out the carousel titles and sorted the words and adjectives into stacks: mode, time, and vibe. Some strays remained, so we created three more stacks: popularity, recency, and curation. By then, Maureen Bee, the Content & Discovery Senior UX Researcher, was excited to connect the latest discovery research to a structured, ML-oriented solution. We continued collaborating closely on the next steps.

The sorting exercise showed that the words represented signals pointing to an attribute of the movie or show. By combining attributes, we could group titles into containers that matched the user’s decision-making progress. Because these attributes appeared in the container’s title, users would understand them. Combining attributes made it possible to “hook” users. Could we create our own internal “hook” system for generating new containers based on the Audience’s Tree of Choice?

The signals (or attributes) were our building blocks, but not all were equal. Our research showed users recognized a hierarchy among attributes, with time at the top. The “hook system” needed to respect this ranking. We grouped signals into three levels by urgency, with Level 1 representing time, the highest priority for users.

## Conclusions and impact

### Some food for thought, and considerations on testing

Focused on improving the container algorithm, the Machine Learning team assumed the logic was self-explanatory. Using wide, vague, top-level categories as containers stated the obvious and left nothing to guide or excite users. Finding “Action”, “Romance”, and “Comedy” was expected, but users had no clue about Tubi’s flavor of these generic topics.

The Lead UX Researcher and I presented this framework to the Machine Learning engineering team and others. We offered it as a conversation starter, not a finished proposal. Concepts from this framework entered everyday conversations about Containers’ strategy and, most importantly, opened the door for collaboration between Design and Machine Learning. Until then, the two teams believed dialogue was impossible due to different assumptions and mutual distrust.
Even without a reliable way to A/B test this approach on our Home, especially given Tubi’s maturity at the time, the Machine Learning team found it interesting and wanted to explore it further. I believe it would have been possible to build a test limited to new users with no prior product history by pinning some new “hook” containers at the top, similar to seasonal marketing rows. Then, by splitting traffic into two groups, we could have measured differences in engagement.

The hook containers weren’t implemented, but the teams’ collaboration lived on.
